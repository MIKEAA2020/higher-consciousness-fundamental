You've laid out two framings that are related but not identical, and the tension between them is actually where things get interesting.
The classical attribute list — timeless, spaceless, unchanging, omniscient, omnipotent, perfect, immaterial — is the "God of the philosophers." You find it in Aquinas, Avicenna, Maimonides. It's built mostly via via negativa: defining the ultimate by stripping away every limitation. And it grounds the ultimate in Being or Pure Act. Consciousness, in that framework, is almost a secondary attribute — something that follows from perfection, not the starting point.
"Consciousness is fundamental" is a genuinely different move. It's closer to:
Advaita Vedanta (Brahman as sat-chit-ananda — being, consciousness, bliss — where consciousness isn't something the ultimate has but what it is)
Berkeleyan idealism
Certain strands of German Idealism (Schelling, Hegel's Absolute Spirit)
Modern analytic idealism (Kastrup, Chalmers' panpsychist leanings)
Some readings of the Upanishads and certain mystical traditions across religions
The reason you might find the second framing "more elegant" is that the attribute list feels like a committee report — a stack of superlatives. Whereas "consciousness is fundamental" is a single ontological claim from which other attributes might derive. If consciousness is the ground, then timelessness and spacelessness follow naturally: time and space are structures within experience, not containers consciousness sits inside. Omnipresence isn't "spread across all locations" but "prior to location itself."
But there are real tensions to wrestle with if you take this premise seriously:
The change problem. If the fundamental consciousness is unchanging, how does the manifest world of change relate to it? Emanation? Illusion? Self-limitation? Each answer has costs.
The individuation problem. If consciousness is one and fundamental, what are you and I? Fragments? Perspectives? Modulations? This is where Advaita, Kashmir Shaivism, and theistic traditions genuinely disagree.
The knowledge problem. An unchanging, timeless knower — does it "know" the way we know? Or is divine knowing something categorically different, where the distinction between knower, knowing, and known collapses?
The grounding problem. Why does a perfect, complete, unchanging consciousness give rise to — or appear as — this? A world of suffering, entropy, partial knowledge? This is the old problem of evil, but sharpened.
Where do you want to take it? Are you building toward a metaphysical argument, exploring a contemplative tradition, trying to reconcile the framework with physics, or something else entirely? The premise you've chosen opens very different doors depending on the direction.

The **Past Hypothesis** is the postulate that the universe began in an extraordinarily low-entropy state, and that this special initial condition is what grounds the thermodynamic arrow of time. It is widely regarded as explanatorily powerful but also deeply unsatisfying, because it appears to be a brute, fine-tuned boundary condition. A number of alternatives have been proposed, ranging from new cosmological dynamics to radical reconceptualizations of time itself.

### 🔄 Dynamical and Cosmological Alternatives

These proposals seek to derive the arrow of time from **typical** dynamics rather than a special initial state.

- **Carroll–Chen model (eternal universe with unbounded entropy).** Sean Carroll and Jennifer Chen propose that the universe is eternal and that the relevant entropy measure is **unbounded**. On this view, entropy does not have a maximum; instead, a typical history has a **U-shaped entropy profile**, with a minimum at some moment and entropy increasing in *both* time directions away from that minimum. Observers on either side of the minimum would identify the direction toward the minimum as “past.” The arrow of time therefore becomes a **typical feature** of universes, not a consequence of an atypical low-entropy beginning.

- **Barbour–Koslowski–Mercati (gravitational complexity).** Julian Barbour and collaborators offer a **relationalist** alternative in which the arrow is not primarily entropic but is tied to a notion of **shape complexity** in a Newtonian gravitating universe. The “Janus point” is the moment of minimal complexity, and the arrow emerges from the growth of complexity in both directions.

- **Weak gravity / phase transition.** Some proposals suggest that the early universe was not low-entropy in an absolute sense; rather, **gravity was weaker** at early times, altering which configurations count as low-entropy. A phase transition that strengthened gravity later could then make the early state appear low-entropy relative to the present.

- **Regime condensation cosmology.** This approach attempts to generate low-entropy initial states as a **structural consequence** of condensation dynamics, without invoking special initial conditions, cycles, or singular boundary states.

- **State-to-state / steady-state cosmology.** Older steady-state models treat all times as statistically equivalent, specifying the universe at any epoch rather than privileging a special past.

### ⚛️ Quantum and Gravitational Alternatives

These approaches locate the origin of the arrow in quantum gravity, quantum cosmology, or the nature of quantum states.

- **Penrose’s Weyl Curvature Hypothesis (WCH).** Roger Penrose argues that the low entropy of the early universe is really a statement about **gravitational entropy**: the initial singularity had **vanishing Weyl curvature**, meaning a highly uniform gravitational field. The WCH is a candidate **quantum-gravity boundary condition** that would explain the low-entropy past as a consequence of deeper physics, rather than as a brute statistical postulate.

- **Bunch–Davies vacuum as the Past Hypothesis.** David Wallace and others have argued that current cosmology points to a unique pure quantum state—the **local quantum vacuum** (Bunch–Davies vacuum)—as the initial state of the universe. On this view, **statistical-mechanical probabilities emerge from quantum probabilities** without any need for an additional statistical postulate. The Past Hypothesis is thereby replaced by a quantum-cosmological boundary condition.

- **Thermal time hypothesis (Rovelli).** Carlo Rovelli’s thermal time hypothesis proposes that the **flow of time is not fundamental** but is **induced by the statistical state** of a system. Time emerges from thermodynamics, rather than thermodynamics needing a special initial time-condition. This dissolves the need for a Past Hypothesis by making temporal flow **state-dependent** rather than a universal background feature.

### 🧠 Philosophical and Epistemic Alternatives

These positions deny that the Past Hypothesis is the right kind of explanation, or that temporal asymmetry is a fundamental physical fact at all.

- **Price’s constructivism / temporal bias.** Huw Price argues that many physical theories **presuppose** the very temporal directionality they claim to explain. He proposes that our sense of a one-way arrow is a **cognitive and linguistic construct**—“temporal bias”—rather than an objective feature of reality. From a “view from outside” time, the laws are symmetric, and the asymmetry is an artifact of our perspective.

- **Humean denial / empiricist rejection.** Some philosophers argue that the Past Hypothesis is **otiose** under a Humean best-system account of laws: it need not be added as a fundamental postulate because the best systematization of regularities does not require it.

- **Layer-phenomenological model.** A more integrative alternative proposes that time has **multiple layers**: a physical layer (entropy, initial conditions), a phenomenal layer (conscious experience of flow), and an existential-conceptual layer. The Past Hypothesis is situated only in the physical layer, and the model aims to avoid reductionism by treating temporal directionality as a **multi-dimensional phenomenon**.

### 🌌 Anthropic and Multiverse Alternatives

These approaches explain the low-entropy past by **selection effects** rather than by a special law or boundary condition.

- **Multiverse plus selection.** If there is a vast multiverse with varying initial conditions, observers will necessarily find themselves in those regions that are hospitable to life—and low-entropy beginnings may be a prerequisite for complex observers. The Past Hypothesis is then replaced by an **anthropic selection effect** operating over a multiverse.

- **Cosmological natural selection.** Lee Smolin’s proposal suggests that universes may “reproduce” through black holes, with slight variations in parameters, leading to a kind of **natural selection** that could favor low-entropy initial conditions.

### ♻️ Cyclic and Bounce Alternatives

These models attempt to explain the low-entropy past as a **recurrent** feature of cosmic cycles.

- **Conformal cyclic cosmology (Penrose).** In Penrose’s CCC, the remote future of one “aeon” becomes the big bang of the next. Black holes evaporate, leaving a universe of pure energy and **vanishing entropy**, which then seeds a new low-entropy beginning.

- **Entropy reset mechanisms.** Some cyclic models propose specific mechanisms—such as **geometric mode depletion** or **superluminal expansion**—that reset entropy at the bounce, yielding a low-entropy initial condition for each new cycle without fine-tuning.
