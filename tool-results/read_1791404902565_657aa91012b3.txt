    10→This is a compressed statement from the philosophy of mind debate about **physicalism, consciousness, and phenomenal concepts**. In plain terms, it means:
    11→
    12→> If you claim that conscious experiences are identical to physical brain states, but that we only discover this identity empirically (“a posteriori”), then you still have to explain why our first-person concepts of experience seem to reveal an inner, non-physical nature.
    13→
    14→Let’s unpack the pieces.
    15→
    16→- **A posteriori identity**: An identity claim known only through experience, not by definition. Example: “water = H₂O.” We don’t know that just by understanding the word “water”; science discovers it. In consciousness, the analogous claim would be: “pain = C-fiber firing” or “red experience = brain state X.”
    17→
    18→- **Phenomenal concepts**: The first-person concepts we use to think about our own experiences—e.g., “this pain,” “what red looks like to me.” They capture *what it is like* to have the experience.
    19→
    20→- **Non-physical essence**: The idea that when you introspect an experience, it seems to present its intrinsic nature as a felt quality—hurtfulness, redness, etc.—not as a neural pattern. It seems to reveal what the experience *essentially is*, and that essence appears non-physical.
    21→
    22→So the challenge is:  
    23→If pain really is just a brain state, why does the phenomenal concept of pain seem to reveal pain’s essence as something non-physical? Why doesn’t introspection present it as a brain state? Why does consciousness seem to have an immaterial inner nature?
    24→
    25→The sentence is saying that **a posteriori identity theory cannot just assert “mind = brain, but we learn it empirically.”** It must also answer this “New Challenge”: explain why phenomenal concepts seem to disclose a non-physical essence. Possible responses:
    26→
    27→1. **Phenomenal concept strategy**: Phenomenal concepts are special first-person concepts that refer to brain states but don’t reveal their physical nature. So the “non-physical” appearance is misleading but explicable.
    28→2. **Illusionism**: The seeming is an illusion; consciousness doesn’t really have a non-physical essence.
    29→3. **Dualism**: The seeming is veridical—consciousness really does have a non-physical essence—so physicalism is false.
    30→
    31→So the quote means: **For the a posteriori identity view to work for consciousness, it must solve the problem of why introspection seems to reveal an immaterial essence.** This is often associated with David Chalmers and the “phenomenal concept” debate. It’s not a neutral description; it’s a demand or objection that physicalists must address.

   250→- They do not entail the existence of a concrete world. From “2+2=4” you cannot derive “there is a universe.”
   251→- They do not explain why there is something rather than nothing.
   252→
   253→So no, mathematical truth is not self-existent **in the order of being**. It does not have aseity in the classical sense—where a being’s essence is to exist and it grounds everything else.
   254→

   256→- Mathematical truth gives you **necessity without existence**.
   257→- Physical reality gives you **existence without necessity**.
   258→- A truly ultimate self-sufficient ground would need to unite both: its nature would have to entail its existence, and it would have to be the source of all else.
