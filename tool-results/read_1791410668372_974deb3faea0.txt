    72→                               (Dissociative Identity Disorder, Dissociative Amnesia, Depersonalization/Derealization
    73→                               Disorder, Other Specified Dissociative Disorder, and Unspecified Dissociative Disorder).
    74→                               Dissociative identity disorder in DSM-5 now refers to possession and/or identity fragmen-
    75→                               tation, in order to be more aligned with possible cultural differences. Dissociative amnesia
    76→                               includes dissociative fugue as a subtype, since fugue is a rare disorder that always involves
    77→                               amnesia but does not always include confused wandering or loss of personality identity [5].
    78→                               Depersonalization disorder also includes derealization, since the two often co-occur. As far
    79→                               as single symptoms are concerned, severe dissociation has been associated with childhood
    80→                               trauma exposure [6,7] and meta-analyses have proved that all forms of childhood traumas
    81→                               are directly associated with dissociation in patients with mental disorders [6]. In fact, pa-
    82→                               tients affected by dissociative identity disorder (DID) report severe childhood traumas [8,9],
    83→                               while patients suffering from depersonalization disorder (DPD) report lower childhood
    84→                               trauma exposure, also compared to patients with depressive disorders [10,11]. It is unclear
    85→                               whether similar disorders with different severity may share neural correlates and etiology,
    86→                               but findings on neural alterations may help solve this puzzle.
    87→                                     In the last four decades, neuroimaging studies have focused on dissociative disorders,
    88→                               suggesting the existence of particular brain activation patterns in patients belonging to
    89→                               this diagnostic category. These studies have shown regional cerebral blood flow (rCBF) or
    90→                               glucose metabolism (CMRglu) changes in the temporal, parietal (implicated in dissociative
    91→                               and conversion phenomena), and orbitofrontal (likely involved in the integration of the
    92→                               self) cortices, in the hippocampus (involved in memory and in the removal of traumatic
    93→                               events), in the basal ganglia, and in other areas. However, they have reached different,
    94→                               and at times conflicting, conclusions, finding out hyperactivation of brain areas in some
    95→                               cases [12–14], and hypoactivation in others [15]. It is also unclear whether these changes
    96→                               involve more often the left or right hemisphere, albeit recent evidence suggests greater
    97→                               damage of the former. Furthermore, most of these studies are limited by low sample size,
    98→                               heterogeneity of diagnosis and comorbidity, and stage of disease (onset, acute, or chronic).
    99→                                     Established models have linked dissociation with increased recruitment in regions
   100→                               involved in executive control [16,17], such as the ventromedial prefrontal cortex, anterior
   101→                               cingulate cortex, and inferior frontal gyri, resulting in dampened activation of the amygdala.
   102→                               Attention has also been focused on the connectivity of temporal cortices, specifically the
   103→                               temporoparietal junctions, because of its involvement in the neurophysiology of multisen-
   104→                               sory integration [18], whose processing can be altered in case of out of body experiences
   105→                               during brain stimulation and lesion studies [19,20].
   106→                                     We hypothesized that there could be common functional changes in patients with a
   107→                               DSM diagnosis of dissociative disorders across different brain regions during task execution
   108→                               and/or during resting state, and that these alterations would match impaired performance
   109→                               of neurocognitive tasks. Considering the low prevalence of dissociative disorders, the
   110→                               low number of functional neuroimaging studies and their mostly inconsistent results, we
   111→                               aimed to clarify the main neural functional pattern associated with these diagnoses. This
   112→                               review aimed to identify alterations in functional neuroimaging techniques reports, and to
   113→                               correlate them with impaired neurocognitive performance and psychopathology of patients
   114→                               with DDs.

   116→                               2. Materials and Methods
   117→                                    A search was conducted using the international scientific database PubMed (http://
   118→                               www.pubmed.gov; accessed on 2 August 2022) to identify functional neuroimaging studies
   119→                               conducted on subjects with a diagnosis of dissociative disorder (i.e., dissociative identity
   120→                               disorder, depersonalization disorder, dissociative amnesia, and other or not specified
   121→                               dissociative disorders). We followed the methods of the Preferred Reporting Items for
   122→                               Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA Statement) [21]. First, we searched the
   123→                               database PubMed with the title/abstract filter, using the keywords “dissociative disord*”,
   124→                               “dissociative amnesia”, “identity disorder”, “PET”, “MRI”, “positron emission”, “functional
   125→J. Pers. Med. 2022, 12, 1405                                                                                            3 of 14
   126→
   127→
   128→
   129→
   130→                               magnetic”, and “spectroscopy.” Second, we collected further studies by examining the
   131→                               bibliographies of relevant articles in the first step or through the “related article” function
   132→                               of the PubMed database, and bibliographies of reviews on the topic.
   133→                                     We included articles on functional neuroimaging studies in samples of patients diag-
   134→                               nosed with dissociative disorders (Dissociative Identity Disorder, Dissociative Amnesia,
   135→                               Depersonalization/Derealization Disorder, Other Specified Dissociative Disorder, and Un-
   136→                               specified Dissociative Disorder) according to the DSM criteria, regardless of the presence
   137→                               of healthy controls in the study. We excluded case reports, case studies, pharmacological
   138→                               trials, reviews, studies on children, studies employing techniques other than functional
   139→                               neuroimaging, and studies involving patients with other psychiatric diagnoses. Three
   140→                               reviewers screened each record and each report reviewed, and collected reported data,
   141→                               working independently from each other, and subsequently verifying together the appropri-
   142→                               ateness of the procedure, and proofreading the included data and results. Two reviewers
   143→                               independently assessed the risk of bias in each included study, considering the sample
   144→                               number, the inclusion/exclusion criteria of participants, the consistency of the sample, and
   145→                               the methods employed, and used the Scottish Intercollegiate Guidelines Network (SIGN)
   146→                               quality assessment tool [22]. We did not register and prepare this study on the basis of a
   147→                               pre-established protocol. We did not use automation tools in our methodological processes.
   148→                                     Based on these criteria, we analyzed 61 studies, from which we excluded 48 studies, of
   149→                               which 26 were unrelated to the subject, 6 were case reports/case studies [15,23–27], 1 was
   150→                               focused on schizophrenia [28], 1 was conducted on adolescents with post-traumatic stress
   151→                               disorder [29], 1 was conducted on healthy subjects [30], 7 used structural neuroimaging
   152→                               techniques [31–37], and 3 were not focused on dissociative disorders [38–40]. We finally
   153→                               included 13 studies published between 2006 and 2022 [14,33,34,41–50]. We grouped the
   154→                               studies in our discussion on the basis of the most consistently reported dysfunctional brain
   155→                               areas in affected patients.
   156→                                     The main study outcomes for which data were sought were changes in neural cortical
   157→                               and subcortical activation patterns and changes in neural metabolism correlated with the
   158→                               diagnosis of a dissociative disorder and/or with symptom severity in patients with a
   159→                               dissociative disorder.

   161→                               Quality Assessment Tool and Risk of Bias
   162→                                    The potential methodological quality of the study and risk of bias were examined
   163→                               through the SIGN quality assessment tool [22]. All studies addressed an appropriate and
   164→                               clearly focused question. Two studies had a high risk of bias in terms of study subjects,
   165→                               because they included two different diagnoses in the sample (view Table 1). In all 13 studies,
   166→                               nevertheless, the two groups being analyzed are selected from source populations that
   167→                               are comparable in all respects other than the factor under investigation. Even when rare
   168→                               differences were present (i.e., age), they were not statistically significant. There were
   169→                               no dropouts in the studies assessed, but no study specified how many of the people
   170→                               asked to take part did so, in each of the groups being studied. The outcomes are clearly
   171→                               defined in every study, the method of assessment of diagnosis is very reliable in each case
   172→                               (DSM), and all psychiatric diagnoses are established through these criteria worldwide.
   173→                               Confidence intervals have always been provided when analyzing brain functional changes
   174→                               in subjects. In conclusion, the biggest risk of bias lies only in the low sample of the
   175→                               studies, which could exaggerate the significance of the results [51]; nevertheless, given
   176→                               the low incidence of dissociative disorders in the general population, the results can be
   177→                               considered reliable. Taking into account clinical considerations, the evaluation of the
   178→                               methodology used, and the statistical power of the study, there is evidence of association
   179→                               between the disease and certain brain functional changes. In addition, according to the
   180→                               Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) system
   181→                               of rating the quality of evidence and grading strength of recommendations in systematic
   182→                               reviews [52], also taking into considerations the two aforementioned studies (view Table 1)
   183→                               which included two different diagnoses in the subject group, and therefore have a higher
