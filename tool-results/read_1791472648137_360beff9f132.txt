    18→Individual variability has clear effects upon the outcome of therapies and treatment approaches. The customization
    19→of healthcare options to the individual patient should accordingly improve treatment results. We propose a novel
    20→approach to brain interventions based on personalized brain network models derived from non-invasive structural
    21→data of individual patients. Along the example of a patient with bitemporal epilepsy, we show step by step how to

    28→Introduction
    29→Personalized medicine proposes the customization of healthcare
    30→with medical decisions, practices and products being tailored to the
    31→individual patient. Individual variability has clear effects upon the re-
    32→sponsiveness to treatment approaches, thus diagnostic testing is often
    33→employed for selecting appropriate and optimal therapies based on
    34→the context of a patient's genetic content or other molecular and cellular
    35→analysis. Historically personalized medicine uses heavily genetic
    36→information, but ﬁnds more and more viability on the systems level.
    37→Structural and functional neuroimaging play a key role and have already
    38→contributed concrete diagnostic tools that are though mostly restricted
    39→to neurology, e.g., such as presurgical evaluation of epilepsy or differen-
    40→tial diagnosis of coma. Other domains such as psychiatry suffer from a
    41→void of diagnostic tools for routine clinical practice.
    42→One solution to this issue that has been proposed by several groups is
    43→to link the interpretation of neuroimaging signals to computational brain
    44→models (Deco et al., 2011; Friston et al., 2014; Jirsa et al., 2010; Stephan
    45→and Mathys, 2014; Stephan et al., 2015). (Jirsa et al., 2002) proposed
    46→the use of connectivity (later referred to as the connectome, Sporns et
    47→al., 2005) derived from Diffusion-weighted MRI (dMRI) to constrain
    48→large-scale brain network models. An upsurge of connectome-based
    49→model development followed with applications to the resting state
    50→(Deco et al., 2009; Ghosh et al., 2008; Honey et al., 2007), aging
    51→(Nakagawa et al., 2013), and pathologies such as schizophrenia (Deco
    52→and Kringelbach, 2014) and lesions (Falcon et al., 2015). So far, modeling
    53→has focused on reproducing the set of functionally active links between
    54→brain areas (the so-called functional connectivity), but has been ham-
    55→pered by the stationary nature of most connectivity-based metrics ap-
    56→plied to validate the models (Hansen et al., 2014). In fact, most
    57→meaningful situations and tasks in neuroscience pose themselves as
    58→non-stationary processes including the resting state, as well as cognitive
    59→and motor behaviors (Allen et al., 2012; Hansen et al., 2014). The same
    60→applies to pathological behaviors also, of which seizure recruitment, the
    61→focus of the current article, is only one example.
    62→Here we argue that large-scale brain network models may make the
    63→link between non-stationary network dynamics (such as seizure propa-
    64→gation) and person-speciﬁc structural indicators including connectivity.
    65→We take advantage of two recent developments in system neuroscience
    66→that is adding Connectomics to Genomics in personalized medicine, and
    67→using patient-speciﬁc connectomes in large-scale brain networks as
    68→generative models of neuroimaging signals. To successfully make this
    69→link, three requirements need to be satisﬁed:
    70→1. Demonstration of systematic and reproducible variation of structural
    71→connectomes across subjects (Bernhardt et al., 2013; Besson et al.,

    98→derivation of the well-known resting state network patterns from
    99→connectome-based models in spontaneous conditions (Hansen
   100→et al., 2014) and following stimulation (Spiegler et al., in press).
   101→This link justiﬁes clustering of patients into subpopulations of clinical
   102→functional relevance.
   103→There is thus evidence indicating that all three criteria may indeed
   104→be satisﬁed, at least for certain cases including epilepsy and lesions
   105→and careful choice of the validation metrics. Under these conditions a
   106→virtualization strategy needs to be established, which is the objective
   107→of this article, and systematically validated. We present our systematic
   108→virtualization approach of an individual patient's brain along the exam-
   109→ple of epilepsy spread and deﬁne the Virtual Epileptic Patient (VEP)
   110→model, thereby identifying the key challenges along its way. All steps
   111→can be performed within the neuroinformatics platform The Virtual
   112→Brain (see http://www.thevirtualbrain.org; (Sanz Leon et al., 2013,
   113→2015 Spiegler and Jirsa, 2013)) and its associated pipelines (Schirner
   114→et al., 2015; Proix et al., 2016).
   115→Our approach to build the VEP brain model comprises the following
   116→steps:
   117→i) Structural network modeling: non-invasive structural neuroim-
   118→aging using MRI and dMRI allows the reconstruction of the
   119→patient's individual brain network topography and connection
   120→topology within the 3D physical space.
   121→ii) Functional network modeling: Neural population models are de-
   122→ﬁned on each network node. For epilepsy, our preferred model is
   123→the Epileptor, which comprises variables for the fast discharges
   124→and the slow energetic processes. Couplings between Epileptors
   125→are deﬁned using the patient's connectome, that is the complete
   126→set of reconstructed white matter tracts
   127→iii) Hypothesis formulation: Structural anomalies such as hamartoma,
   128→pachygyria, etc. (as observed for instance in the MRI) are identi-
   129→ﬁed within the network. Non-invasive functional neuroimaging
   130→further informs the expert clinician on the evolution of the
   131→epileptic seizure and allows the formulation of ﬁrst hypotheses
   132→of the location of the Epileptogenic Zone (EZ), here deﬁned as
   133→the hypothetical area in the brain responsible for the origin
   134→and early organization of the epileptic activity (Talairach and
   135→Bancaud, 1966). The Propagation Zone (PZ) comprises areas that
   136→are recruited during the seizure evolution, but are by themselves
   137→not epileptogenic. Parameters (epileptogenicity, anomalies) are
   138→set in the network model following the hypothesis on EZ
   139→(Bartolomei et al., 2013).
   140→iv) Evaluation of the VEP brain model: The patient's brain network
   141→model is evaluated via simulation, data ﬁtting and mathematical
   142→analysis. It can be used to either “ﬁngerprint” individual patient
   143→brains by identifying a personalized parameter set through data
   144→ﬁtting or according to clinical criterion. Systematic computational
   145→simulations will further generate parameter maps outlining the
   146→zones of seizures and seizure freedom. These maps will give guid-
   147→ance of how to tune model parameters (patient charts). The result
   148→of this evaluation predicts the most likely propagation patterns
   149→through the patient's brain and allows the exploration of brain
   150→intervention strategies.
   151→In the following we demonstrate all steps necessary to build a VEP
   152→model for a particular patient. We simulate and analyze the VEP
   153→model. In particular we show parametric analyses of the VEP model
   154→and ﬁt it against functional stereotactic EEG (SEEG) data. Finally we
   155→discuss the limits of its interpretation and point out future avenues for
   156→VEP modeling.
   157→Materials and methods
   158→Patient data
   159→The patient is a right-handed 41-year-old female initially diagnosed
   160→with bitemporal epilepsy. The patient underwent comprehensive
   161→presurgical evaluation, including clinical history, neurological examina-
   162→tion, neuropsychological testing, structural and diffusion MRI scanning,
   163→EEG and stereotactic EEG (SEEG) recordings along with video monitor-
   164→ing. Nine SEEG electrodes were placed in critical regions (see Table 1)
   165→based on the presurgical evaluation. SEEG electrodes comprise 10 to
   166→15 contacts. Each contact is 2 mm of length, 0.8 mm in diameter and
   167→is 1.5 mm apart from other contacts. Brain signals were recorded
   168→using a 128-channel Deltamed™system (sampling rate: 512 Hz, hard-
   169→ware band-pass ﬁltering: between 0.16 and 97 Hz).
   170→Structural and diffusion MRI were acquired with a Siemens
   171→Magnetom Verio 3 T MR-Scanner Siemens, Erlangen, Germany).
   172→T1-weighted images were acquired with a MPRAGE-sequence (TR =
   173→1900 ms, TE = 2.19 ms, voxel size =1 × 1 × 1 mm3, 208 slices). The
   174→diffusion acquisition used a DTI-MR sequence (angular gradient set of
   175→64 directions, TR = 10.7 s, TE = 95 ms, 70 slices, voxel size =
   176→2 × 2 × 2 mm3, b-value = 1000 s/mm2).
   177→Structural reconstruction
   178→The large-scale connectivity and the cortical surface of the patient
   179→were reconstructed using SCRIPTS (available on GitHub: https://
   180→github.com/timpx/scripts), a processing pipeline tailored for TVB
   181→(Proix et al., 2016). The pipeline uses FreeSurfer (Fischl, 2012), FSL
   182→(Jenkinson et al., 2012), remesher (Fuhrmann et al., 2010), and MRtrix
   183→(Smith et al., 2013, 2012; Tournier et al., 2007) to process T1 and
   184→dMRI scans. The brain is divided in several regions according to a
   185→parcellation template, which is used for whole brain tractography to
   186→develop the connectivity (number of streamlines) and delay (length of
   187→streamlines) matrices. Cortical and subcortical surfaces are reconstruct-
   188→ed and downsampled, along with a mapping of vertices to correspond-
   189→ing region labels. All processed data are formatted to facilitate import
   190→into TVB.

   233→time scales. On the fastest time scale, state variables x1 and y1 account
   234→for the fast discharges during the seizure. On the slowest time scale,
   235→the permittivity state variable z accounts for slow processes such as

   417→Anomalies in the MRI
   418→Structural anomalies will require their integration into the model.
   419→Here we included a hypothalamic hamartoma in our virtualization via
   420→a modiﬁcation of the local connectivity Kij=GhypCij of the hypothala-
   421→mus. We delineated the hamartoma in the MRI scan and tractography
   422→and used the hypothalamic hamartoma as a seed region of interest to
   423→reconstruct its local connectivity. The local connectivity strength is
   424→scaled up parametrically by a scalar factor Ghyp to quantify the effect of
   425→the hamartoma without changing its local connection topology.
   426→Data ﬁtting
   427→The target for all data ﬁtting is the excitability parameter x0, which
   428→may be set either to clinical criterion (see the Virtual Brain based
   429→simulations, EZ and PZ section) or estimated using more automated
   430→approaches. Obtaining such estimates of the parameters of the network
   431→model, given the available functional data is performed within a
   432→Bayesian framework, using a reduced Epileptor model (Proix et al.,
   433→2014) and reduced functional data set for the ﬁtting. The SEEG data
   434→are windowed and Fourier transformed to obtain estimates of their
   435→spectral density over time (Bricolo et al., 1978). Then SEEG power
   436→above 10 Hz is summed to capture the temporal variation of the
   437→fast activity. These time series are corrected to a preictal baseline,
   438→log-transformed and linearly detrended over the time window
   439→encompassing the seizure (Makeig, 1993). Contacts are selected,
   440→which present greater high-frequency activity than their neighbors on
   441→the same electrode. Given that, contrary to M/EEG, the SEEG lead ﬁeld
   442→is very sparse, three nodes per contact are used in the network model.
   443→Other nodes are not recruited and rest at their ﬁxed points. We approx-
   444→imate this effect, in the ﬁtting only, through a constant sum over the cor-
   445→responding elements of the structural connectivity matrix. These
   446→elements correspond to areas, which neither participate in the seizure,
   447→nor have nearby depth electrodes and are thus observable only through
   448→network effects, whose hidden states prove uninformative and unreli-
   449→able in their estimation. Next, we use an observation model that
   450→incorporates the SEEG forward solution described above, under the as-
   451→sumption that the x1 variable describes ﬂuctuations in the log power
   452→of high frequency activity, predicting sensor log power, with normally
   453→distributed observation error.
   454→Hidden states in Bayesian modeling represent states of the genera-
   455→tive model (variables, parameters) that are not directly observable.
   456→Uninformative priors are placed on the hidden states' initial conditions,
   457→while their evolution follows a Euler-Maruyama discretization of the
   458→corresponding stochastic differential equations with linear additive
   459→normally distributed noise. Uninformative priors are also placed on
   460→the excitability parameter per node x0 observation baseline power,
   461→scale and noise. Finally the length of the seizure is also allowed to freely
   462→vary to match that of a given recorded seizure. Structural connectivity
   463→speciﬁes a gamma prior on the connectivity used in the generative
   464→method. This model is implemented using Stan, a software for Bayesian
   465→inference, which implements both Hamiltonian Monte-Carlo and auto-
   466→matic variational inference algorithms for generic differential probabil-
   467→ity models (Hoffman and Gelman, 2011; The Stan Development Team,
   468→2015). This approach takes advantage of the efﬁciency of the variational
   469→algorithm, which constructs an approximate proxy distribution on the
   470→true posterior optimized via stochastic gradient ascent (Kucukelbir
   471→et al., 2015).
   472→Numerical methods
   473→To simulate the system of stochastic differential equations, we used
   474→an Euler-Maruyama integration scheme with an integration step of 0.05.
   475→Additive white Gaussian noise was introduced in the variables x2 and y2
   476→with mean 0 and variance 0.0025 (Jirsa et al., 2014). Other variables ex-
   477→perienced only little or no noise due to their high sensitivity. 256 time
   478→steps are equivalent to one second of real time to obtain realistic
   479→frequency ranges, seizure lengths, and matched intracranial EEG
   480→sampling frequency. Whenever speciﬁed in the Results section, we
   481→used a bandpass Butterworth ﬁlter of order 5, with cut-off frequencies
   482→of 0.16 Hz and 97 Hz at −3 dB (Fig. 2D). The ﬁlter is identical to the
   483→one used to process intracranial EEG signals (see (Bartolomei et al.,
   484→2008) for processing methods of intracranial EEG signals). Finally, for
   485→the stimulated seizure, a rectangular function in time was applied on
   486→the z variable of the stimulated region (amplitude: 0.5, length: 2 s).

   556→Virtual Brain based simulations, EZ and PZ
   557→Each node of the network was occupied with an Epileptor, a neural
   558→mass model able to reproduce epileptic seizures dynamics (Jirsa et al.,
   559→2014). For the Epileptor in isolation (i.e. no network coupling), if
   560→x0Nx0C, the neural mass is above threshold: the Epileptor can trigger sei-
   561→zures and is said epileptogenic, and inversely if, x0bx0C, the Epileptor
   562→does not trigger seizure and is said not epileptogenic. When embedded
   563→into a network, each network node's capacity of triggering seizures will
   564→then depend on its excitability and connectivity. The nodes were con-
   565→nected via permittivity coupling, which acts on a slow time scale and
   566→allow the spread of the seizure through the network by recruiting re-
   567→gions not in the EZ. In this section we set the excitability parameters
   568→for EZ, PZ and all other regions according to clinical criteria comprising
   569→(i) regions involved in the seizure; (ii) seizure length; (iii) length of
   570→time delays before recruitment of other regions; (iv) seizure frequency
   571→in each region. These criteria are used clinically in standard-of-care to
   572→evaluate the epileptogenicity of brain regions (Bartolomei et al., 2008,
   573→Proix et al., 2014). The spatial distribution of excitability was heteroge-
   574→neous across the network (see Fig. 1), with high value of excitability for
   575→regions in the EZ (x0≥x0C+0.4), smaller excitability values for regions
   576→in the PZ (x0C+0.4Nx0≥x0C), and other nodes not epileptogenic
   577→(x0bx0C).
